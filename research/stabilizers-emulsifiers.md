# Stabilizers, emulsifiers, and related additives: evidence note

## Scope and evidence convention

This note supports a short, cited education tab and ingredient guidance in the
Ice Cream Formula Calculator. It focuses on home-scale dairy ice cream and
frozen desserts, with particular attention to carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), guar
gum, lambda carrageenan, lecithin, collagen, and xanthan gum.

Superscript references point to the numbered sources below. Underbelly is a
carefully researched culinary source, but it is not peer reviewed. Its formulas
are therefore described as **practical starting points**. Claims described as
**published evidence** come from journal articles or reviews. An **inference**
is a proposed home-use interpretation that has not been tested as that exact
formula.

## Short explanation suitable for the app

**Stabilizers** thicken or structure the unfrozen water phase. Their most
important long-term role is slowing ice recrystallization during storage and
temperature fluctuation. They also change body, melt, and flavor release. They
do not all work through the same mechanism, and increased mix viscosity alone
does not fully explain their effects.³⁻⁵,¹⁹

**Emulsifiers** do something different. In ice cream, they help partially
destabilize the milk-fat emulsion during freezing. This controlled partial
coalescence helps create the fat network around air cells and influences
whipping, dryness, shape retention, and melting.⁸⁻¹⁰,¹⁷,²⁰ Egg yolk supplies
phospholipids and proteins, including lecithin. Lecithin can also be added
without egg.²

**More is not better.** Excess stabilizer can make the melt gummy, elastic,
sticky, pasty, or slow to release flavor. Excess emulsifier can produce a
greasy or curdy texture and may impair whipping.¹,²,³,¹⁰ The useful amount
depends on fat, protein, sugar, total solids, pH, processing, storage, and the
specific grade of the additive.³,¹⁸

As broad conventional-ice-cream context, the University of Guelph formulation
guide lists 0–0.4% stabilizer and 0–0.25% emulsifier, and describes about
0.2–0.5% stabilizer plus emulsifier combined.²¹,²² These are formulation
envelopes, not targets for every recipe or for a Ninja CREAMi.

Published experiments generally support a stronger effect on *recrystallization
during storage* than on the size of the first crystals produced during dynamic
freezing.⁴,⁵ The app should not promise that a gum automatically creates smaller
initial ice crystals.

## Ingredient guide

| Ingredient | Main contribution | Processing and cautions | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CMC / cellulose gum** | Strong storage-recrystallization control with body and chew; useful in cold-process mixes. | Underbelly describes cold hydration and warns that CMC can form gels in combinations with LBG, guar, or carrageenan.¹ Published ice-cream studies support CMC as a functional stabilizer, but do not establish a universally best dose.⁴,⁶,¹⁶ | Direct ice-cream research plus culinary processing guidance |
| **Guar gum** | Cold-hydrating viscosity, body, and some elasticity; often paired with another gum. | It hydrates without heat but may take time to reach full viscosity. Too much can make the frozen product chewy or elastic.¹ LBG and guar both inhibit recrystallization, but their relative performance depends on concentration and the model system.⁴,⁵ | Direct ice-cream research plus culinary processing guidance |
| **Lambda carrageenan** | Primarily tunes creamy melted texture and can help control dairy-protein separation (“wheying off”). | Lambda is non-gelling compared with kappa and iota carrageenan and Underbelly describes it as rapidly hydrating at room temperature.¹ Carrageenan also had a particularly temperature-dependent effect on ice-cream-mix viscosity in an earlier comparison.⁷ A mixture-design ice-cream study found higher viscosity with lambda than iota at the same proportion, while iota generally gave stronger melting resistance.²⁴ This supports distinct roles, not one-for-one substitution. | Direct but limited lambda-specific ice-cream research plus culinary guidance |
| **Locust bean gum (LBG)** | Strong recrystallization control with a comparatively unobtrusive, creamy texture; a frequent base for multi-gum blends. | Most grades require heating, approximately 74–85 °C according to supplier grade. It can form gels with xanthan, CMC, kappa carrageenan, or iota carrageenan.¹ LBG was more effective than guar in one series of model and ice-cream experiments, but no gum is best in every system.⁴,⁵,¹⁸ | Direct ice-cream research plus culinary processing guidance |
| **Xanthan gum** | Easy cold hydration, shear-thinning body, and robust function across temperature and acidity. | Convenient for no-cook use. High levels can feel slimy. It forms a gel with LBG, so that pair is not a neutral substitute for two independently acting gums.¹ Xanthan performed strongly against recrystallization in a milk-protein model.⁵ In matched-viscosity low-fat ice cream, xanthan's more rigid network was harder to scoop and was perceived as colder and grittier than flexible LBG or guar systems.²³ | Direct model/ice-cream research plus culinary guidance |
| **Gelatin** | Weak, body-temperature-melting gel; adds frozen body and can be easy to use at home. | Animal-derived. Underbelly heats it to about 60 °C and combines it with xanthan.¹ In one protein-containing frozen model, gelatin did not retard recrystallization as effectively as the tested polysaccharides, so it should not be presented as the strongest anti-iciness option.⁵ | Direct frozen-model evidence plus culinary guidance |
| **Collagen peptides / hydrolyzed collagen** | Protein solids; selected low-molecular-weight peptide fractions can inhibit ice crystal growth or modify melt and texture. | Ordinary retail collagen powder is **not equivalent to gelatin** and does not form the same gel. Ice-structuring activity depended on peptide molecular-weight range and production method.¹¹,¹² One chocolate-ice-cream study favored 0.2% specific porcine-skin hydrolysate plus 0.3% gelatin, not collagen alone.¹³ Treat consumer collagen as experimental unless its functional specification is known. | Direct research on specially produced peptide fractions; weak generalizability to retail powders |
| **Egg yolk** | Natural emulsification from yolk phospholipids and proteins; at higher levels, cooked yolk also thickens and adds custard body. | Underbelly's practical guidance distinguishes about 0.5–1% yolk for emulsification from roughly 3–4% or more for noticeable thickening.² These are culinary estimates, not a universal optimum. Egg changes fat, solids, color, flavor, and allergen status as well as emulsification. | Strong culinary precedent and mechanistic support; dose guidance mainly practical |
| **Soy or sunflower lecithin** | Egg-free phospholipid emulsifier; useful when a formula lacks yolk or contains added fat from chocolate or nuts. | Underbelly suggests 0.15–0.45% of total mix as an egg-free range and cautions that excessive lecithin can impair whipping.² A controlled low-fat-ice-cream study found lecithin improved body and texture but produced the lowest flavor scores among the emulsifiers tested.¹⁰ Source and grade therefore matter, especially for people with soy or egg allergies. | Direct low-fat-ice-cream evidence plus culinary dose guidance |
| **Mono- and diglycerides / polysorbate 80** | Efficient commercial emulsifiers for fat destabilization, whipping, and structural control. | Useful at very low concentrations, but usually unnecessary for a dense home Creami-style product unless a specific structural problem is being solved. Underbelly gives approximate starting concentrations of 0.1–0.2% for mono-/diglycerides and 0.02–0.04% for polysorbate 80.² Emulsifier performance is not interchangeable.⁸,¹⁰ | Direct ice-cream research; home dose guidance partly culinary |
| **Cornstarch or tapioca starch** | Accessible cooked thickener and body builder. | Requires heat. Starch generally has a more noticeable texture and less clean flavor release than small-dose gums. Underbelly regards tapioca as more freeze-thaw tolerant than cornstarch but provides no single universal dose.¹ | Culinary guidance; not reviewed in depth here |
| **Inulin** | Soluble fiber and body/fat-replacement ingredient rather than a primary emulsifier. | At percent-level doses it changes total solids and nutrition, so it should not share a milligram-scale gum control. A response-surface study found 3% inulin could replace part of the fat while increasing viscosity and producing acceptable texture.¹⁴ | Direct ice-cream research |
| **Milk powder / whey proteins** | Adds milk solids, protein, water-binding capacity, and native emulsifying surfaces. Heat treatment can alter protein functionality. | This is part of formula balancing, not a drop-in gum. Protein level and heat history affect fat destabilization and structure, so it should remain a normal ingredient with protein/MSNF accounting.²,⁹ | Direct dairy-emulsion research and culinary guidance |
| **Kappa or iota carrageenan; sodium alginate** | Stronger gelling options that can add body in low-fat products. | Calcium-containing dairy can promote gel formation. This can be useful, but can also create a base too firm to process. A controlled study tested CMC, guar, alginate, or xanthan with kappa carrageenan at a 9:1 ratio and 0.1% or 0.2% total hydrocolloid.⁶ Do not substitute kappa or iota one-for-one for lambda. | Direct ice-cream research |
| **Tara gum** | A galactomannan option whose culinary behavior is described as intermediate between LBG and guar. | Usually heat hydrated and less common at home. It is a reasonable “other option,” but should not be marketed as literally identical to a 50:50 LBG/guar blend without product-specific testing.¹ | Primarily culinary guidance |

## Combinations worth presenting

Amounts should be calculated from **total mix mass**, not nominal tub volume.
For example, 0.15% means 1.5 g per 1,000 g of mix. A 709 mL tub does not
necessarily contain exactly 709 g of every formula.

### 1. Heated general-purpose dairy blend

- **LBG : guar : lambda carrageenan = 4 : 2 : 1**
- Underbelly example: **0.8 g + 0.4 g + 0.2 g per litre**, described as about
  **0.15% of the total formula**.¹
- Role split: LBG emphasizes recrystallization control, guar adds frozen body,
  and lambda carrageenan enriches the melted texture and helps with protein
  stability.¹
- Requires heating to the hydration temperature of the LBG grade.¹
- **Interpretation:** a sensible culinary default for a cooked dairy or
  low-yolk base. It is not a peer-reviewed universal optimum.

### 2. Eggless heated dairy blend with lecithin

- **Lecithin : LBG : guar : lambda carrageenan = 4 : 4 : 2 : 1**
- Underbelly example: **1.0 g + 1.0 g + 0.5 g + 0.25 g per litre**.¹
- The lecithin replaces some of egg yolk's emulsifying role while the gum
  concentration is higher than in the low-yolk general-purpose example.¹,²
- **Interpretation:** a useful egg-free starting preset, especially for
  chocolate or nut formulas, but the exact dose must be tuned to fat, protein,
  and desired overrun.

### 3. Cold-process CMC, guar, and lambda carrageenan

- **CMC : guar : lambda carrageenan = 2 : 1 : 1**
- Underbelly's published example is **2 g + 1 g + 1 g per litre**, expressed as
  **0.5% of formula water**, and is explicitly a **sorbet** blend.¹
- CMC emphasizes cold-process recrystallization control, guar adds body, and
  lambda carrageenan enriches the melt.¹
- **Important:** do not silently make the sorbet concentration the default for
  dairy ice cream. Underbelly also warns that CMC can form gels with guar and
  carrageenan.¹ Direct dairy studies support CMC, guar, and carrageenan as
  stabilizers, but do not validate this exact lambda-carrageenan ternary blend
  at this exact dose.⁴,⁶,¹⁶
- **Home-use inference:** offer this as an experimental cold-process preset with
  an explicit texture warning, or let the user choose the three ingredients
  individually. A lower total concentration may be appropriate in dairy, but
  this review does not establish a validated dairy starting percentage.

### 4. Easy-to-find gelatin and xanthan

- **Gelatin : xanthan = 3 : 1**
- Underbelly example: **1.0 g + 0.33 g per litre**, about **0.15% total**.¹
- Gelatin contributes cold/frozen body. Xanthan contributes more to the melted
  viscosity. Too much xanthan becomes slimy.¹
- **Interpretation:** an accessible home option, not the strongest
  evidence-based choice for storage recrystallization.⁵

### 5. Egg yolk plus a low-dose gum blend

- Underbelly's standard-base example combines **two large yolks per litre**
  with the 4:2:1 LBG/guar/lambda blend at about 0.15% total mix.¹⁵
- The yolk supplies emulsification and some custard character. The gums handle
  more of the storage stability, allowing fewer yolks than a heavy French
  custard.¹,²,¹⁵
- **Interpretation:** a good interface preset is “light custard + stabilizer,”
  distinct from both “egg-free” and “full custard.”

### 6. Collagen plus gelatin: research example, not a general preset

- A study of a specific low-molecular-weight porcine collagen hydrolysate in
  chocolate ice cream found the most favorable tested combination was **0.2%
  hydrolysate + 0.3% gelatin**.¹³
- Other studies found only selected gelatin/collagen peptide fractions
  inhibited ice crystal growth.¹¹,¹²
- **Interpretation:** show collagen peptides under “experimental protein
  additives,” not as a direct substitute for gelatin, CMC, or xanthan. There is
  no direct evidence here for a special collagen-plus-xanthan synergy.

## Practical instructions and guardrails

1. **Use a 0.01 g scale.** Gum amounts are too small for reliable teaspoon
   conversion.¹
2. **Preblend powders thoroughly.** Mix gums into sugar and other dry
   ingredients before adding them to liquid, then use adequate shear to avoid
   clumps.¹
3. **Follow the specific grade's hydration instructions.** Underbelly reports
   rapid room-temperature hydration for lambda carrageenan, slower cold
   hydration for guar, cold hydration for CMC and xanthan, about 60 °C for
   gelatin, and roughly 74–85 °C for LBG depending on grade.¹ Supplier
   specifications override generic guidance.
4. **Separate dispersion from hydration.** A powder can look evenly dispersed
   before it has developed its final viscosity. The app should state whether a
   selected system needs heat and/or an aging period.¹
5. **Count additives already present in ingredients.** Commercial cream, cream
   cheese, plant milks, protein powders, and pudding mixes may already contain
   carrageenan, guar, CMC, xanthan, lecithin, or starch. Unknown carryover makes
   fine control difficult.
6. **Tune one blend at a time.** Excessive viscosity can reduce air
   incorporation, while excessive emulsification can cause greasy texture,
   curdy melt, or poor whipping.²,³,¹⁰,¹⁶
7. **Evaluate the finish, not only scoopability.** Gumminess, elasticity,
   stickiness, pastiness, or a mouth-coating finish are signs to reduce the
   total dose or change the blend.¹,³
8. **Do not use a single “best stabilizer” badge.** Direct studies and a recent
   methodological review show that apparent recrystallization performance
   changes with concentration, milk protein, freezing method, cycling
   conditions, measurement method, and polysaccharide structure.⁴,⁵,¹⁸,²³
9. **Flag source constraints.** Egg and soy lecithin can matter to people with
   those allergies. Gelatin and collagen are animal-derived. Sunflower lecithin
   avoids soy but is not automatically performance-equivalent across
   commercial grades.

## Recommended information architecture for the education tab

Keep the first view short:

1. **What are stabilizers and emulsifiers?** Use the two short definitions
   above.
2. **Choose by goal:** “less icy after storage,” “creamier melt,” “more body,”
   “egg-free emulsification,” “cold process,” and “low-fat body.”
3. **Common combinations:** show the heated LBG/guar/lambda, eggless lecithin
   blend, cold CMC blend, gelatin/xanthan blend, and light-custard blend as
   separate cards.
4. **Ingredient library:** use the table above in expandable cards. Always show
   heat requirement, role, principal caution, and evidence label.
5. **References:** use numbered superscripts in the cards and a compact
   reference list below.

In the calculator itself, keep **stabilizer total**, **emulsifier total**, and
**body-building solids** separate. Inulin, collagen powder, and milk powder
should not be added to the milligram-scale “gum total,” and lecithin should not
be counted as a stabilizer merely because it is an additive.

## Evidence gaps to disclose

- No selected published study validates the exact CMC/guar/**lambda**
  carrageenan combination as an optimal dairy-ice-cream blend. Underbelly's
  exact 2:1:1 formula is for sorbet.¹
- Hydration behavior and viscosity differ by molecular weight, substitution,
  purification, and supplier grade. Ingredient names alone are insufficient for
  exact predictions.
- Much of the controlled literature uses churned, homogenized ice cream. A
  Ninja CREAMi freezes a static tub and later shaves/processes it. The direction
  of the ingredient effects is useful, but published doses are not
  machine-specific optima.
- Collagen-peptide findings apply to characterized hydrolysates or fractions,
  not automatically to any retail collagen supplement.¹¹⁻¹³
- The user-facing tab should present dosage as a starting point with a stated
  basis (percent of total mix mass or percent of formula water), never as a
  safety limit or guaranteed optimum.
